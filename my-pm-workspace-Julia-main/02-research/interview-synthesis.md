# User Interview Synthesis — Streak Comeback Experience

*Source: 3 interviews — Priya S. (power user, 14-month streak), Tom R. (churned user, broke a 12-day streak at 5 weeks in), Amara L. (new user, 4 days in).*

## Top 5 Themes

### 1. Milestone celebration builds long-term commitment
Once the app acknowledged sustained progress, that recognition became the reason to protect the streak going forward.

- Priya: "The thing that made it stick was hitting a 30-day streak and the app actually celebrating it, that little moment of 'look what you built.'"
- Priya: "After that I did not want to lose it."

### 2. Habit formation takes time, and most users don't survive long enough to reach it
The payoff (ritual, celebration) comes weeks in — but the fragile early window is where users are most likely to quit.

- Priya: "it took me about three weeks to get there, and I think most people quit long before the habit forms."
- Amara: "I don't want to lose everything I've built after four days."

### 3. Streak-loss anxiety exists even before any lapse happens
The pressure isn't only a reaction to breaking a streak — it's present from the earliest days, independent of an actual lapse.

- Amara: "I'm already stressed about the streak, I keep thinking, what happens if I miss a day?"
- Amara: "the pressure is starting to feel like a chore instead of a game."

### 4. The zero-reset after a lapse feels punishing and irreversible
When a streak does break, the experience offers no acknowledgment of what was built and no way back in.

- Tom: "I traveled for work, missed two days, and came back to a big fat zero."
- Tom: "There was no way to recover it, nothing. So I gave up."

### 5. Harsh notification tone and rigid mechanics push users toward more forgiving competitors
The messaging around a lapse compounds the emotional hit, and users have a forgiving alternative to compare against.

- Tom: "The app sent me this 'you lost your streak' notification that just made me feel bad."
- Tom: "I switched to Duolingo, at least there a missed day doesn't wipe everything, and a streak freeze feels forgiving."

## Contradictions / Tensions

- **The same mechanic cuts both ways.** For Priya, the streak (and its celebration) is what created commitment. For Tom, that same all-or-nothing mechanic is exactly what ended his use of the app. The streak is either the glue or the breaking point, depending on whether it survives intact.
- **Anxiety precedes any actual lapse.** Amara is stressed about losing her streak on day 4 — before anything has gone wrong. This suggests the punishing design isn't only a problem *after* a break (Tom's experience); it's already shaping how new users feel about the product *before* a break (Amara's experience).
- **Single data point on the competitive contrast.** Tom's comparison to Duolingo's streak-freeze supports the team's working hypothesis, but it's one churned user's account, not corroborated by the other two interviews.

## Single Most Important Insight for Week-1 Retention

The anxiety that ultimately drives churn doesn't start at the moment of a break — it starts almost immediately, well before the ~3-week mark Priya describes as when the habit actually forms. Amara, only 4 days in, is already dreading a lapse she hasn't had yet. That means a fix aimed only at the *comeback* moment (reactive, after a break) addresses Tom's experience but misses Amara's — the fix also needs a *proactive* component in week 1 itself: reducing loss-aversion pressure before a user ever breaks a streak, not just softening the landing after they do.
